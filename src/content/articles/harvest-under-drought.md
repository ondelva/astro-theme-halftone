The Verrow Main Canal was built sixty years ago to carry snowmelt from the highlands down through a grid of feeder channels that once turned the plain into the region's most reliable grain belt. This year, for the third season running, long stretches of it carry nothing at all.

Farmer Basim Okwuosa walks the dry bed near his family's plot most mornings, checking a stretch that has not run since early May. "My grandfather helped dig this channel," he said. "He never imagined a year it would stay empty this long."

The Verrow Basin Water Authority allocates canal water on a rotating schedule among the plain's roughly four thousand registered farms. This year's allocation has been cut to a third of the historical average, forcing growers to choose which fields to irrigate and which to let go fallow.

Okwuosa planted half his usual acreage and is watching even that shrink. His maize stands knee-high in patches and barely ankle-high in others, depending on which end of his plot the last rotation reached.

"You learn to read the water like a ration card," he said. "Some weeks you get your turn. Some weeks the turn doesn't come."

Downstream, at the plain's central packing shed, manager Liesel Foppe has cut seasonal hiring by nearly two-thirds. The shed once employed close to two hundred workers during harvest. This year it kept seventy.

"There's no fruit to pack if there's no water to grow it," she said. "The drought doesn't just hit the farm. It hits everyone downstream of the farm too."

The Water Authority has proposed lining sections of the canal to cut seepage losses, a measure engineers estimate could recover close to fifteen percent of current flow. The project has been discussed for two years without securing the funding to begin.

Authority director Casimir Wren said the agency was doing what it could within an allocation set by rainfall it does not control. "We can manage the water we have more efficiently," he said. "We cannot conjure water that never fell."

For Okwuosa, the arithmetic is simpler and more immediate. He has begun selling off a portion of his equipment, a decision he says he never expected to make on a farm his family has worked for three generations. He still walks the canal most mornings, less from habit than from something closer to hope that one day the water will be running when he gets there.
