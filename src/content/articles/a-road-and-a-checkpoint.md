There is one paved road between Corvale and the coast, and for the last eleven kilometres of it, traffic funnels into a single inspection bay at Dray Ford before it can go any further. On a Tuesday in June, the queue started at the old grain silo, more than four kilometres back.

Dray Ford has controlled movement across the Kelder tributary since a checkpoint was first built there in the 1980s, but the volume it handles today bears little resemblance to what the original single-bay design was built for. Regional traffic counts show daily crossings have more than doubled in a decade, while the inspection infrastructure has not changed.

"It's one bay, one officer on most shifts, and everybody from produce trucks to school buses goes through the same lane," said driver Costin Marek, who hauls vegetables from Corvale to coastal markets three times a week. "I plan my whole day around how bad the line is going to be."

## Three hours, on a good day

Marek's logbook, which he keeps partly for his own sanity and partly for insurance purposes, records an average wait of just under three hours over the past month, with a worst case of five hours on a day when the inspection equipment went down entirely.

The checkpoint sits under the authority of the regional transport ministry, which acknowledged the delays in a statement but pointed to a planned second bay as evidence the problem is being addressed. Construction has not yet started, and the ministry's own timeline lists a start date more than a year away.

For Corvale's farmers, the wait is not an inconvenience so much as a cost. Produce that sits in a truck bed for three extra hours in summer heat loses value before it reaches market.

"I've had crates I had to sell at half price because they wilted in the queue," said grower Halina Tovar. "That's not the checkpoint's fault directly. It's what the checkpoint does to a truck full of lettuce."

## Living by the line

Some drivers have adapted by leaving before dawn, when the queue is shortest, arriving at Dray Ford by four in the morning to be through by seven. Others pay a local broker who tracks queue length by radio and calls ahead with an estimate.

Checkpoint supervisor Oskar Lundqvist, who has worked the bay for nine years, said staff do what they can within the constraints of one lane and a workforce that has not grown with the traffic.

"We're not slow because we want to be," he said. "You put a thousand vehicles a day through one gate, that gate becomes the whole story of the road."

The transport ministry's proposed second bay would roughly double throughput, according to the engineering study behind it, but the project remains unfunded beyond a preliminary design phase. Marek, for his part, has stopped waiting on the ministry's timeline and instead keeps his logbook, tracking a queue that has not gotten shorter in the three years he has been recording it.
