The diner on Ostrell's Fort Street opens at four in the morning to catch the first wave of haulers waiting for the Kelder crossing to open. This September, owner Delphine Osei counted eleven trucks in her lot instead of the usual thirty.

"I used to run out of coffee by six," she said. "Now I run out of things to do."

Ostrell sits on the Kelder River, across from the Tarnholm side, and has depended on the crossing since a bridge replaced the old ferry landing in the 1960s. Warehousing, toll collection, truck repair and a strip of motels built around one fact: freight moving between the two sides has to pass through this town.

In April the Frontier Authority replaced its two-lane cargo inspection with a single lane, arguing that consolidated screening would cut fraud and speed the average crossing time once the new equipment settled in. Instead, the change created a bottleneck that haulers now route around.

## The detour nobody announced

Freight companies that once scheduled Ostrell crossings as routine have shifted a portion of their loads to the Halden crossing, ninety minutes north, where inspection still runs two lanes. The Ostrell Chamber of Commerce estimates the town has lost close to two thousand truck crossings a month since the change.

"Sixty-two percent of the jobs in this town touch the crossing in some way," said chamber director Wynn Castellanos. "Toll clerks, mechanics, the motels, my own members who sell diesel. You take a third of the trucks away and you take a third of the wages away with them."

The Frontier Authority disputes that its rule is the cause. Regional director Petra Solheim said in a written statement that average wait times had in fact fallen for trucks that completed inspection, and that any drop in Ostrell's volume reflected broader freight patterns rather than the lane change.

Toll records tell a more specific story. The average toll paid per truck at Ostrell rose from $1.75 to $4.10 over the same period, a figure the Frontier Authority attributes to a fee restructuring passed alongside the inspection change and unrelated to traffic volume. Haulers see it differently.

"They raised the price and slowed the line," said Iker Manns, who has driven the Kelder route for eleven years. "I go where it's faster and it isn't Ostrell anymore."

## What the town does now

Osei has cut her overnight staff from three to one. The Fort Street motel down the block has closed its west wing. Castellanos has asked the regional government for a subsidy tied to crossing volume, similar to one already paid to Ostrell's smaller rival crossing forty miles south, but says the request has not moved since June.

Not everyone in Ostrell blames the Authority alone. Mechanic Reuben Tarquah, who has kept his shop open by taking on general repair work beyond trucks, said the town had leaned too hard on one road for too long.

"We built a whole town on people driving through it," he said. "Maybe that was always going to run out."

Solheim said the Authority would review lane capacity again in the new year, and that a second inspection lane remained possible if traffic data supported it. For Osei, the timeline matters less than the count on any given morning. She has started keeping her own tally on a chalkboard by the register: trucks in, trucks gone. In September the board read, more often than not, in the single digits before sunrise.
